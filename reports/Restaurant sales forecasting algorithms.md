# Simple seasonal forecasts beat clever models here

The best forecasting algorithm for Maginhawa is not one algorithm. It is a **robust same-weekday baseline, adjusted by bookings on the books and bank-holiday rules, with a likely range calibrated from the method's own past errors**, all hand-written in TypeScript in `packages/core`. That is essentially the team's agreed method ("same weekday over the last 6 weeks, nudged by bookings and the recent trend"), made precise and testable. The evidence for keeping it simple is strong. In the M5 competition on Walmart daily sales, **only 35.8% of teams beat a seasonal naive forecast and only 7.5% beat bottom-up exponential smoothing** ([M5 paper](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/M5_accuracy_competition.pdf)). On the one large public restaurant benchmark, Kaggle's Recruit (821 restaurants), the **winning ensemble was only about 27% better than seasonal naive and 50th place was 25% better** ([Bojer & Meldgaard](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.07701)), so most of the achievable gain comes from a sound, well-fed model rather than an exotic one. The global LightGBM models that power DoorDash and the M5 winners earn their edge from thousands of related series. Maginhawa has 27 (9 sites × lunch, dinner, delivery), and M5's gains **shrank to about 3% at the most granular levels** ([M5 paper](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/M5_accuracy_competition.pdf)). The plan below therefore ships an explainable v1 now, starts logging the data a better v2 needs (especially "as-of" bookings snapshots), and promotes any challenger model, including gradient boosting or a foundation model, only if it beats v1 in a walk-forward backtest. Realistic expectations: roughly 10–25% lower error than "same weekday last week", with **no credible published figure for absolute site-level restaurant accuracy**. Vendor claims of "95% accurate" are marketing.

A note on labels: claims marked as **evidence** come from published results with sources linked. Claims marked as **inference** are reasoning applied to Maginhawa's situation and must be confirmed against the group's own backtests.

## Competitions show seasonal baselines are hard to beat

The forecasting competitions point the same way, and they point away from complexity for small, short, strongly weekly series. **M4** (100,000 series) was won by Smyl's hybrid of exponential smoothing and neural networks, but the winning margin over simple benchmarks was **under 10%**, and "the combination of methods was the king of the M4" ([Fast ES-RNN summary](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.03329); [M4 results](https://pure.unic.ac.cy/en/publications/the-m4-competition-results-findings-conclusion-and-way-forward/)). M4 also contained only 4,227 daily series and no exogenous variables, so it says little directly about restaurants ([Bojer & Meldgaard](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.07701)).

**M5** (42,840 hierarchical Walmart daily series, 28 days ahead) was the first M competition won by pure machine learning: an **equal-weighted average of LightGBM models trained across many series**, beating the best statistical benchmark by **22.4%** ([M5 paper](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/M5_accuracy_competition.pdf)). Three details matter more for Maginhawa than the headline. First, the top 50 improved on exponential smoothing by about **40% at the total level but only about 3% at product-store level**, so the gains came from aggregation and cross-learning across thousands of series. Second, exogenous information helped even simple models: exponential smoothing with event and promotion regressors was **6% better** than without them. Third, many teams **failed to pick their own best submission** because of misleading validation scores; had they done so, 48.8% rather than 35.8% would have beaten seasonal naive ([M5 paper](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/M5_accuracy_competition.pdf)). The lesson is that validation discipline matters as much as model choice.

**Kaggle Recruit Restaurant**, the closest analogue, forecast daily visitors 1–39 days ahead from 15 months of data and advance reservations. The winner averaged LightGBM, XGBoost and feed-forward neural networks using rolling statistics and **lagged reservations**. A traditional **Kalman filter placed 33rd, only 2.4% behind first**, and the authors noted that "more traditional time series models can still be viable with exogenous variables". Treating holidays as Saturdays and adjacent days as Fridays or Mondays "generally gave a significant performance boost" ([Bojer & Meldgaard](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.07701)). Across six Kaggle forecasting competitions, **ensembles won every time** ([same source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.07701)).

Small restaurant studies broadly agree. A 2025 study of four German restaurants (hourly sales, 14 days ahead, two years of history) found gradient boosting best overall, **Chronos-Bolt zero-shot matching it at 3 of 4 sites, and Prophet underperforming** ([Arab & Benitez](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.03395)). A customer-count study found XGBoost best, with previous-day count, exponential-smoothing outputs, holidays, weekday and weather as the key features ([AIMS ACI 2025](https://www.aimspress.com/aimspress-data/aci/2025/2/PDF/aci-05-02-011.pdf)). Counter-evidence exists: a Bangladesh restaurant study found simple exponential smoothing beat XGBoost ([PMC12136425](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12136425/)), and a European meal-delivery study found **exponential smoothing optimal below a daily-volume threshold**, with machine learning competitive only above it, given about two months of history ([Hess, Spinler & Winkenbach](https://code.webartifex.biz/alexander/urban-meal-delivery-demand-forecasting/raw/tag/v0.1.0/paper.pdf)).

Foundation models (Chronos, TimesFM, TimeGPT) are the contested frontier. Their authors' benchmarks show large skill gains over seasonal naive: on fev-bench, Chronos-2 scores **35.5 MASE skill against AutoETS's 2.3** ([fev-bench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.26468)). Independent work with leakage controls finds the reverse: statistical models and their ensembles **beat foundation models by more than 8.2% on sCRPS and more than 20% on MASE** across 15 competition datasets ([Olivares, Wolff et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.19465)), and SCUM, a median of four statistical models, beat Chronos by about 10% using a fifth of the compute on Nixtla's selection ([Baron & Karpinski](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.10216)). **Inference:** a foundation model is a credible low-effort benchmark, especially for a new site with only a few weeks of history, but it is not reliably better than a tuned statistical ensemble and cannot use bookings unless a covariate-capable variant is used.

## Big platforms use pooled gradient boosting, but 27 series changes the maths

Industry practice at scale has converged on **one global gradient-boosted tree model across many related series**. DoorDash forecasts supply and demand for thousands of ZIP-code groupings with LightGBM because it trains thousands of regional forecasts in one run, and adds a **linear "cascade" layer for holidays** because tree models struggle on rare, high-variance days ([DoorDash holiday cascade](https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/how-doordash-improves-holiday-predictions-via-cascade-ml-approach/); [DoorDash LightGBM post](https://careersatdoordash.com/?p=5078); both read via search extract). Amazon's research lineage (DeepAR, MQ-CNN) produces direct P10/P50/P90 forecasts across tens of thousands of products ([MQ-RNN paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.11053)). Uber's forecasting guide lists ARIMA, Holt-Winters and Theta alongside machine learning, insists on **always reporting prediction intervals**, and recommends comparing every method against a seasonal-naive benchmark in chronological backtests ([Uber, 2018](https://www.uber.com/blog/forecasting-introduction/)). Restaurant chains themselves publish almost nothing; Domino's UK & Ireland's move to Dynamics 365 demand planning is a vendor case study ([Microsoft](https://microsoft.com/en/customers/story/1698105041618606470-dominos-microsoft-dynamics-365-united-kingdom)).

Restaurant-tech vendors all describe "AI/ML on POS history plus weather, holidays and events", and none publishes a reproducible method.

| Vendor | Claim | What it is measured against |
|---|---|---|
| Tenzo (UK) | "30–50% more accurate"; manager adjustment adds "a further 5%"; needs 90 days | Gut feel or a four-week rolling average ([Tenzo](https://www.gotenzo.com/forecast-demand); [support](https://support.gotenzo.com/docs/forecasting-your-future-sales/)) |
| 7shifts | "95% accuracy"; 3.61% average daily error in 2022 | Metric undefined, likely aggregated ([7shifts KB](https://kb.7shifts.com/hc/en-us/articles/14620377028627), via search extract) |

Two useful facts sit inside the marketing. Tenzo's own guide names the **four-week same-weekday rolling average** as the most common manual method ([Tenzo guide](https://www.gotenzo.com/resources/insight/sales-forecasting-methods-a-comprehensive-guide-for-restaurants/)), which makes it the natural baseline for Maginhawa's backtest. Lineup.ai's claim implies that about **a third of restaurant days land more than 20% away from that average** ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20201209005074/en)), which is the gap that holiday rules, bookings and weather are meant to close.

**Inference:** at 27 series, the cross-learning advantage that won M5 is much smaller, but its principle still applies. Pooling across the nine sites is the cleanest way to give a new site with six weeks of history a stable weekday shape, holiday effect and error band. That pooling can be done with simple shrinkage towards group averages; it does not require LightGBM.

## Bookings and bank holidays matter more than the algorithm

Recruit's distinctive input was advance reservations, and hotel revenue management has studied the same problem for decades. Weatherford and Kimes found **pickup methods and regression had the lowest error on Choice Hotels data**, and exponential smoothing, pickup and moving averages were most robust on Marriott data ([Weatherford & Kimes 2003](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/bitstream/handle/1813/72130/Kimes30_Comparison_of_Forecasting.pdf)). Pickup is a one-line formula: forecast covers equal covers booked at lead time L plus the historical average of (final minus booked at L) for that weekday and lead time, or a multiplicative version. It explains itself to a manager ("42 booked, Thursdays usually add 18 walk-ins at this point").

Bank holidays are cheap and reliable to encode. The gov.uk JSON feed lists England and Wales bank holidays, including substitute days, **through to 26 December 2028** as fetched on 27 October 2026 ([gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/bank-holidays.json)). The Recruit "holiday as Saturday" trick and DoorDash's separate holiday layer both say the same thing: rare days need explicit rules, not hope that a model learns them.

Weather evidence is mixed, mostly American and often from vendors. Ohio State researchers found **rain did not affect sales but temperature did, non-linearly**, with lunch the most temperature-sensitive meal ([OSU](https://news.osu.edu/was-the-restaurant-really-that-bad--or-was-it-just-the-rain/); [Restaurant Dive](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/fair-weather-diners-how-temperature-influences-consumer-cravings/549897/)). UK industry commentary summarised it as "sunny → pub; wet → restaurants" ([The Caterer](https://www.thecaterer.com/news/restaurant/bad-weather-affects-sales-at-pub-and-restaurant-groups)), and Tenzo reports that **temperature relative to the seasonal norm**, not raw temperature, drives deviations ([Tenzo blog](https://www.gotenzo.com/?p=1383)). **Inference:** weather probably matters most for delivery and footfall-led lunch, least for booked dinners, and its effect for Maginhawa must be measured on its own data before it is trusted.

Data sources carry practical constraints. **Open-Meteo's free tier is non-commercial only** ([Open-Meteo pricing](https://open-meteo.com/en/pricing)), and an internal operations app for a restaurant group is commercial use. The **Met Office Weather DataHub free tier allows 360 site-specific calls a day**, with paid tiers from £8 a month ([DataHub pricing](https://datahub.metoffice.gov.uk/pricing/site-specific)), which comfortably covers nine sites. London school term dates have no national feed; boroughs publish their own, and academies may differ ([Wandsworth](https://wandsworth.gov.uk/termdates)). **Inference:** a head-office table of term dates, one row a year, is sufficient.

## Likely ranges should come from the method's own past errors

Uber's guidance is that two identical point forecasts can carry very different uncertainty, and that wider intervals imply more reserve capacity ([Uber, 2018](https://www.uber.com/blog/forecasting-introduction/)). The platforms publish quantiles (P10, P50, P90) and staff to the upper ones; Instacart uses 0.9-quantile regression for delivery-time upper bounds ([Instacart](https://tech.instacart.com/how-instacart-delivers-on-time-using-quantile-regression-2383e2e03edb)). Restaurant vendors mostly show a single number.

The method that suits Maginhawa is **conformal prediction on rolling-origin residuals**, which works with any point forecaster and needs no distributional assumptions. Run the v1 forecaster in a walk-forward backtest, collect its errors per service and horizon as relative errors (actual ÷ forecast − 1), and take empirical quantiles with the conformal (n+1) correction to form an 80% band. Then apply **adaptive conformal inference (ACI)**, which re-tunes the miscoverage level online after each day's actual arrives and gives **long-run coverage guarantees under distribution shift** ([Gibbs & Candès, NeurIPS 2021](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2021/hash/0d441de75945e5acbc865406fc9a2559-Abstract.html)); a follow-up tunes the step size so it reacts faster ([Gibbs & Candès 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.08401v2)). Conformalised quantile regression gives tighter, heteroscedasticity-aware bands ([Romano et al.](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/5103c3584b063c431bd1268e9b5e76fb-Paper.pdf)) and EnbPI wraps bootstrap ensembles ([Xu & Xie](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.09107v15)), but both only pay off once a gradient-boosted or ensemble model exists.

Sample size is the binding constraint. **Inference, based on standard conformal arithmetic:** an 80% split-conformal interval needs at least 4 residuals to be finite, and coverage only stabilises within a few percentage points at around 50–100 residuals. Six weeks of history gives about six residuals per site, service and weekday. So v1 should pool relative errors across weekdays and across sites, use horizon buckets (1–3, 4–7, 8–14 days) rather than 14 separate pools, show 80% rather than 95% bands (easier for managers and less data-hungry), and deliberately widen bands until each pool holds 20 or more residuals. The day-total range should be calibrated from day-total backtest errors directly, not by adding up the service bands, because service errors are correlated.

Scoring follows from this. Hyndman and Koehler showed that many common accuracy measures "degenerate in commonly occurring situations" and proposed **MASE**, which scales error by a naive benchmark so it compares fairly across sites of different size ([Hyndman & Koehler](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/mshebswps/2005-13.htm)). **Inference:** MAPE is unstable on near-zero days (a quiet delivery day, a closed lunch), so it should not be the main metric.

| Metric | Use | Who sees it |
|---|---|---|
| MASE (denominator: same weekday last week, lag 7) | Model selection across sites and services; below 1 beats the naive baseline | Developers, head office |
| WAPE (Σ\|error\| ÷ Σ actual) | Plain "the forecast was x% out this week" in £ terms; safe with zeros | Managers |
| Bias (mean error) | Catches systematic over- or under-staffing | Head office |
| Interval coverage and mean width | Did 80% of actuals land in the 80% band, and how wide was it | Developers |
| Pinball loss / Winkler score | One number rewarding both calibration and narrowness | Developers |

## From trading desks, borrow the hygiene and leave the signals

Quant finance transfers through its statistical discipline, not its alpha. Combining forecasts has the strongest evidence: in M4, **12 of the 17 most accurate methods were combinations** ([M4 results](https://pure.unic.ac.cy/en/publications/the-m4-competition-results-findings-conclusion-and-way-forward/)), and optimised weights often fail to beat equal weights (the "forecast combination puzzle", [arXiv 2308.05263](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.05263)). Pesaran and Timmermann found that **averaging the same model over different estimation windows reduces error when there are structural breaks** ([J. Econometrics 2007](https://rady.ucsd.edu/_files/faculty-research/timmermann/estimation-window.pdf)), which directly answers "how much history should a site use after a refurb?".

Backtest-overfitting control matters too. Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado and Zhu show hold-out validation is "unreliable and inaccurate" when many configurations are tried ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2326253)), and M5's selection failures are the demand-forecasting proof of the same point. The equivalent of trading's look-ahead bias is using final bookings or actual weather in a backtest. **Using weather forecasts rather than observed weather in model selection improved accuracy by 2.4%** in load forecasting ([UNC Charlotte](https://graduateschool.charlotte.edu/short-term-ex-ante-load-forecasting/)). **Inference:** Maginhawa must store bookings "as known at the time" each night from day one, or it can never honestly test a bookings model.

State-space thinking transfers well. The Kalman local-level model is, in steady state, **equivalent to simple exponential smoothing (EWMA)** ([arXiv 2512.16745](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.16745)), so a structural model with local level, weekly seasonal and regressors for bookings and holidays is a natural v2 that handles closures (skip the update) and known interventions (inflate the level variance). For monitoring, CUSUM tracking signals on standardised forecast errors have been used for demand forecasts since **Harrison and Davies in 1964** ([Tracking signal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tracking_signal); [Gardner 1985](https://www.bauer.uh.edu/egardner/3301H%20Operations%20Management/ESG%20Publications/1985%20Cusum%20vs.%20smoothed%20error.pdf)); Bayesian online change-point detection is a principled alternative with no retail application found ([Adams & MacKay](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0710.3742)).

What does not transfer: momentum and random-walk-centred modelling, because stock returns are close to unpredictable ([Lo & MacKinlay](https://web.mit.edu/~alo/www/Papers/lo-mackinlay-88.html)) while restaurant sales are dominated by stable weekly seasonality. No study was found showing momentum features help sales forecasts beyond a level component. GARCH has only adjacent evidence in safety-stock work ([UPV](https://riunet.upv.es/handle/10251/159356?show=full)); empirical residual quantiles do the same job more simply. High-frequency microstructure methods have no counterpart at daily granularity.

## Twenty-seven series fit in milliseconds of TypeScript

**Inference from the sizes involved:** 27 series × 14 days is 378 forecast points a day, from at most about 30,000 historical numbers. A weekday median, Holt-Winters, pickup and a 26-week rolling backtest all run in well under a second in plain TypeScript. Netlify **scheduled functions have a 30-second limit, background functions 15 minutes, and the London (`lhr`) region is available on Pro and Enterprise** ([Netlify](https://docs.netlify.com/build/functions/configuration.md)), which helps with UK GDPR data residency. The real latency risk is reading SharePoint through Graph, not the maths.

The JavaScript forecasting ecosystem is thin. The `arima` npm package is a WASM port of a C library supporting SARIMAX and AutoARIMA, but it has a **third-party health score of 54/100** ([npm](https://www.npmjs.com/package/arima); [depscope](https://depscope.dev/pkg/npm/arima)) and is a black box. **Inference:** hand-writing about 300 lines of pure functions (seasonal naive, weekday median, ETS/Holt-Winters with a small parameter grid, pickup, conformal quantiles, ACI, metrics) gives full explainability and golden-fixture Vitest tests, matching the project rule that calculations live in one tested `packages/core` module.

Hosted options are cheap but carry other costs. **Amazon Forecast is closed to new customers** ([AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/forecast/latest/dg/API_Types.html)). Google's AutoML forecasting prediction costs **$0.20 per 1,000 data points** for the first million a month, and BigQuery's `AI.FORECAST` embeds TimesFM ([Vertex pricing](https://cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/pricing?hl=he); [BigQuery docs](https://docs.cloud.google.com/bigquery/docs/reference/standard-sql/bigqueryml-syntax-ai-forecast)), so at Maginhawa's volume the bill would be a few pounds a month. TimeGPT is available on Azure Foundry, billed in the customer's own subscription ([Nixtla](https://www.nixtla.io/blog/timegpt-azure-marketplace)), though current TimeGPT-2.x prices were not confirmed. **Inference:** the real costs are data leaving the Microsoft 365 tenant (a DPIA point, though sales data is mostly not personal data), US default regions, lock-in and "the model said so" explanations. Python's `statsforecast` (AutoETS, AutoARIMA, built-in conformal intervals), run nightly in a GitHub Action, is the best free *benchmark* for checking that the TypeScript model is close to state of the art.

## Recommendation: ship v1 now, earn v2 with data

### v1 (February 2027 launch, from 6 weeks of history)

| Component | Rule | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline | Per site × service: **median of the last 6 same weekdays**, excluding flagged closures and bank holidays; shrink towards the group weekday shape while a site has fewer than about 12 weeks | Evidence: seasonal baselines strong (M5, Recruit); robust median is inference |
| Trend nudge | Multiply by a **clipped level ratio**: last 14 days' actual ÷ the baseline's forecast for those days, limited to ±10–15% and damped over the horizon | Inference (EWMA / local-level logic); clip limits to be tuned in the backtest |
| Bank holidays | Treat as the site's own Sunday (or Saturday, per site) with a group multiplier until each site has its own history; flag the day before and after | Evidence: Recruit holiday trick, DoorDash holiday layer |
| Bookings (dinner) | Additive pickup: covers booked at lead L + historical average pickup for that weekday and lead; **blend with the baseline** until enough as-of snapshots exist | Evidence: Weatherford & Kimes |
| Sales from covers | Net sales forecast as its own series, cross-checked against covers × recent average spend per head | Inference |
| Manager override | Allow a dated note ("event nearby", "private hire") that adjusts and is logged | Evidence (vendor): Tenzo, Toast |
| Likely range | 80% band from pooled relative backtest errors by service and horizon bucket, conformal (n+1) correction, then ACI after each actual | Evidence: conformal and ACI theory; pooling thresholds are inference |
| Shadow challenger | ETS / Holt-Winters (additive, weekly) on log values, computed but not shown, and an equal-weight average of it with the baseline | Evidence: combinations win (M4, M5) |
| Logging | Store every published forecast and band, and a nightly **as-of snapshot of bookings** for the next 14 days | Inference, essential for honest v2 testing |

The manager-facing explanation stays one sentence: "Based on your last six Thursdays, up 4% on recent weeks, with 42 covers already booked. Eight in ten similar forecasts landed in this range." (Figures illustrative only.)

### v2 (from about 12 months of history and 3+ months of as-of bookings)

Candidates enter a fixed, pre-registered list, so the backtest cannot be gamed by trying dozens of variants. The front-runner is a **structural state-space model in TypeScript** (local level, weekly seasonal, regressors for bookings, bank and school holidays, temperature anomaly and rain from the Met Office forecast), with CUSUM monitoring and manager-entered interventions for refurbs or menu changes. Alongside it, run a **nightly Python benchmark** (`statsforecast` AutoETS/AutoARIMA, and a pooled LightGBM across the 27 series with site and service as categories) and optionally a **foundation model** (TimesFM via BigQuery or TimeGPT on Azure) as a cold-start aid for new sites. Promote to the live forecast an **equal-weight combination** of whichever candidates beat v1, not the single best scorer, following M4/M5 and the combination puzzle. Annual seasonality and year-on-year holiday alignment (Christmas, Easter) only become estimable after a full year.

### Evaluation method

Use **walk-forward (rolling-origin) backtesting** as FPP3 describes: at each origin, fit only on data up to that day, forecast 1–14 days ahead, and score each horizon separately ([FPP3 §5.10](https://otexts.com/fpp3/tscv.html)). Origins should be every day across at least the last 8–12 weeks, more as history grows, and should include holidays where they exist, as M5 did ([M5 paper](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/M5_accuracy_competition.pdf)). Rebuild bookings and weather as they were known at each origin. Compare expanding and rolling windows. Always include two baselines: same weekday last week (seasonal naive) and the four-week same-weekday average. Report the **mean and spread of error across origins**, as the M5 winner did, and hold back the most recent four weeks untouched for the final check. Adopt a model only if it beats seasonal naive on MASE consistently across sites and horizons, with 80% bands covering 75–85% of actuals. After launch, the logged live forecasts become the ongoing scorecard on the head-office Forecast tab.

## Conclusion

The question "which algorithm?" turns out to be the less important one. For nine sites, the accuracy ceiling is set by inputs and discipline: bookings captured as they stood each night, bank holidays encoded as rules, ranges calibrated from honest walk-forward errors, and a short pre-registered list of challengers. The agreed method was right in spirit; what it lacked was a calibrated range, a holiday rule, a clipped trend and a scorecard. The highest-value action this month is not modelling at all: it is starting the nightly as-of bookings snapshot and the forecast log, because every future improvement, and every claim to managers that the forecast can be trusted, depends on data that cannot be recreated after the fact.

The open risk is the start-up period. With six weeks of history, weekday-specific error bands are statistically thin, so the first ranges will be wider than managers expect and should be presented as "will narrow as we learn". Gradient boosting and foundation models remain worth testing once a year of data exists, but the evidence says they should earn their place against a seasonal baseline rather than be assumed superior.
