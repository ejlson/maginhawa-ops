# Close the loop restaurants' apps leave open

The opportunity is a product for **independent restaurant groups with 1–10 locations**. Today no affordable app gives them all four of the pillars they need: opening/closing and food-safety checklists, repairs and maintenance, an employee handbook with policies, and inspection-ready records. Buyers have absorbed the dedicated vendors one after another. Crunchtime bought Zenput and Squadle, Access bought Trail, and Digi bought Jolt for about $145.5M. Each of those products was then pulled toward enterprise suites. Independents are left with $13-per-location task add-ons, horizontal tools priced per user, single-purpose food-safety apps, or paper. Check65 is not a standalone product to copy. It is the free field app of Food Alert's UK consultancy-plus-software bundle (owned by Citation), and its strength is regulatory content and advisory services, not UX or price. Reviews across Jolt, Crunchtime, SafetyCulture and 7shifts all complain about the same things: slow mobile apps, flaky Bluetooth hardware, contract lock-in, heavy setup, and support that "never fixes anything." Those complaints point to how a new entrant wins. Build it phone-first and offline-first. Make it useful in 15 minutes using templates driven by jurisdiction rules. Make it hard to fake without hardware. Price it per location with unlimited users and month-to-month terms, at roughly **$49–$119 per location per month**. The differentiating feature is a closed loop. A failed check becomes a corrective action, then a work order, then a vendor visit, then a cost and warranty history, and all of it rolls up into an "inspector mode" record. No SMB competitor ties these together. The main risks are that independents churn and are shrinking about 2% a year, that POS platforms are moving up-stack with AI, and that exits in this category have priced at mid-single-digit ARR multiples. Several regulatory details below are flagged **[verify]** because the underlying research could not confirm them against a primary source.

## Check65 is a consultancy's field app, and the market around it has split into five camps

Check65 is the mobile companion to **Alert65**, the compliance platform of **Food Alert**, a London food-safety consultancy founded in 1990. The Citation Group bought Food Alert in 2019 and publishes the app as Citation Holdings Limited ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/check65/id1611955849); [Food Alert](https://www.foodalert.com/software/alert65/)). The app covers daily, weekly and monthly checklists (opening/closing, cleaning, fire safety), ad-hoc checklists such as visitor logs, hot, cold and delivery temperature checks, role-based task filtering, a green/amber/red priority score, Bluetooth probes, and offline mode. Alert65 adds the depth behind it: digital HACCP plans, **200+ consultant-written risk assessments**, an allergen matrix, training tracking, certification-deadline monitoring, incident logging, and EHO inspection-readiness tools ([Food Alert](https://www.foodalert.com/software/alert65/)). Food Alert claims **3,000+ food businesses and 1 million checklists a month**. Pricing is quote-only. The iOS app has only **2 US ratings**, so its distribution comes through enterprise contracts and Citation's HR and health-and-safety cross-sell rather than app-store discovery ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/check65/id1611955849)). Two things follow. A newcomer will not out-consult Food Alert on UK regulatory content. And Check65's public feature list has no repairs and maintenance workflow, no handbook, no transparent pricing and no self-serve onboarding. Those four gaps are the openings.

The wider market falls into five camps. US multi-unit ops platforms (Jolt, Crunchtime Ops, Xenia, Operandio, Delightree) are mostly quote-based. Food-safety and HACCP specialists (FoodDocs, ComplianceMate/Ladle, Navitas, Food Alert) carry the IoT and HACCP depth. UK checklist apps (Trail, Culinary Key) publish transparent per-site prices. Horizontal tools (SafetyCulture, Connecteam, MaintainX, UpKeep) charge per user or per hub. Scheduling and back-office suites (7shifts, Homebase, Restaurant365) sell tasks as a cheap add-on. AI-first entrants such as Palona ($20M raised, with Din Tai Fung as a customer) and Owner ($240M Series D) are aiming at voice, vision and agents rather than checklists ([Restaurant Technology News](https://restauranttechnologynews.com/2026/08/palona-ai-expands-beyond-voice-ordering-with-new-restaurant-operations-platform-and-20-million-in-funding/); [Restaurant Business](https://www.restaurantbusinessonline.com/technology/owner-raises-240m-ai-native-restaurant-tech)).

## Operators buy speed and proof, and churn over slow apps, flaky probes and lock-in

Reviews are consistent across vendors. Jolt holds 4.6 on Capterra, yet its users report that the app "can be quite unreliable; it is slow and sometimes freezes up." They also describe onboarding as "overwhelming," call pricing "much higher than its competitors," and warn others to change contract terms "so they aren't locking you in for services that don't work" ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/p/146384/Jolt/reviews/); [Software Advice](https://www.softwareadvice.com/logbook/jolt-profile/reviews/)). Crunchtime Ops reviewers cite slow image uploads, a mobile experience weaker than desktop, Bluetooth problems and "expensive hardware on top of monthly subscriptions." They also ask for more custom reporting and bulk export ([G2](https://g2.com/products/zenput/reviews?page=3)). SafetyCulture users say value depends on staff actually completing photos and notes, otherwise managers end up "chasing things up" ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/p/141080/iAuditor/reviews/)). For 7shifts, a competitor-authored analysis attributes **64%** of negative reviews to bugs and **56%** to unreliable or overwhelming notifications ([Connecteam](https://connecteam.com/best-7shifts-alternatives/)). Treat that source with caution. Praise follows a pattern too: replacing paper, knowing who did what and when, and "the checklist feature saved us from chaos."

The deeper problem is **pencil-whipping**, meaning checklists and temperature logs completed without doing the work. Time pressure drives it, along with weak training and the belief that "no one's ever gotten sick" or that nobody reads the logs ([Crunchtime](https://www.crunchtime.com/blog/how-to-prevent-pencil-whipping); [Ladle](https://ladle.com/resources/blog/mitigate-checklist-falsification-at-your-restaurant-with-one-tool/)). Incumbents counter it with hardware, but hardware is also the top source of complaints, because Bluetooth and Wi-Fi struggle through stainless steel and insulated cold units ([Refrigerated & Frozen Foods](https://www.refrigeratedfrozenfood.com/articles/94163-temperature-humidity-sensors-for-foodservice-kitchens?v=preview)). A product that makes faking difficult with software alone has a real opening. Software alone here means live-capture photos, server timestamps, detection of identical repeated readings, and checklists that auto-close.

The economics justify the pain. The typical US operator spends a **median $22,500 a year on equipment repair and maintenance** ([Bar & Restaurant / 86 Repairs](https://www.barandrestaurant.com/operations/new-report-empowers-operators-make-better-repair-maintenance-spending-decisions)). Almost half of reach-in refrigerator service calls are for units **"running too warm"** ([86 Repairs](https://blog.86repairs.com/the-state-of-repairs-2020-data)). A single walk-in freezer circuit-board failure cost **$23,800**, of which $22,000 was spoiled stock ([HSB/Munich Re](https://www.munichre.com/hsbcanada/en/hsb-insights/restaurants-equipment-breakdown.hsb.html)). The CDC attributes **41% of restaurant outbreaks** with an identified cause to ill or infectious workers and **10.6% to improper cooling** ([ABC7 on CDC](https://abc7ny.com/cdc-report-restaurant-foodborne-illness-sick-workers-study/13320094/)). Hourly turnover runs about **92–110%**, with replacement costing about **$2,706 per hourly worker and $17,651 per GM** ([Netchex](https://www.netchex.com/blog/restaurant-turnover-rate-understanding-costs-benchmarks-and-proven-retention-strategies/)). That turnover rate is why onboarding, handbook acknowledgement and microtraining belong in the same app. Frontline workers agree: **7 in 10 say app-based training would make learning easier** ([Axonify via AAP](https://www.aap.com.au/aapreleases/cision20241029ae42429)). Shift handover is a further gap. Standalone manager-logbook apps such as Avero Logbook and Zip ShiftBook still exist, which shows that handover sits apart from checklists and maintenance today ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1259702980)).

One gap in the research matters. Reddit and forum threads could not be retrieved, so the voice of independent operators comes from review sites and vendor blogs. Before building, the founder should do 20–30 operator interviews and read r/restaurantowners and r/KitchenConfidential directly.

## Regulations reward contemporaneous, reviewable records, and the thresholds vary by jurisdiction

The most important design implication is that **thresholds must never be hard-coded**. In the US the FDA Food Code is a model code that binds a restaurant only once its state or locality adopts it. The current edition is the 2022 Food Code plus a **Supplement published 4 Nov 2024**. The Supplement adds disinfection provisions, food-defense awareness, sushi-rice acidification guidance, and formal definitions of **Food Safety Management System and Active Managerial Control**, including when an FSMS is required ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/hfp-constituent-updates/fda-releases-supplement-2022-food-code)). That last change positions a digital verification tool as the operator's documented FSMS. The core loggable rules are as follows. The danger zone is 41–135°F. Two-stage cooling runs **135→70°F within 2 h, then 70→41°F within a further 4 h** ([ICN](https://theicn.org/resources/521/food-safety-fact-sheets/107110/cooling-foods.pdf)). Date marking holds ready-to-eat TCS food **7 days at ≤41°F, counting prep day as day 1** ([CDC](https://cdc.gov/restaurant-food-safety/php/practices/date-marking.html)). Sesame has been the 9th major allergen since 1 Jan 2023 ([Fox13](https://www.fox13news.com/news/sesame-to-become-9th-major-food-allergen-on-jan-1-fda-says)). **FSMA 204 traceability will not be enforced before 20 July 2028**. When it is, records must be producible as an electronic sortable spreadsheet, with small-business exemptions ([CSP Daily News](https://cspdailynews.com/foodservice/food-traceability-rule-delayed-until-2028); [FDA](https://fda.gov/media/163838/download)).

UK law requires a permanent HACCP-based procedure with records "commensurate with the nature and size" of the business under retained Reg 852/2004 Art 5 ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2004/852/article/5/data.htm?view=plain)). The FSA's Safer Food, Better Business pack **explicitly allows an electronic diary** kept until the next officer visit ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/safer-food-better-business-sfbb/safer-food-better-business-sfbb)). The FHRS 0–5 score includes a "management systems" element that rewards visible, reviewed records. Display of the score is mandatory in Wales and Northern Ireland ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/food-hygiene-rating-scheme-fhrs-guidance-for-businesses/food-hygiene-rating-scheme-fhrs-guidance-for-businesses)). Holding chilled food above **8°C** or hot food for service below **63°C** is an offence ([SI 2013/2996](https://legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/2996/schedule/4)). FSA guidance from March 2025 says **written allergen information should always be available** for non-prepacked food. That is best practice rather than statute ([FSA](https://www.food.gov.uk/news-alerts/news/updated-industry-guidance-issued-for-food-allergen-information-in-the-out-of-home-sector)).

Workplace rules feed the handbook and maintenance modules. US restaurants are **partially exempt from OSHA 300 logs**, but every employer must report fatalities, in-patient hospitalizations, amputations and eye loss ([OSHA](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1904/1904SubpartBAppA)). Harassment training is state-driven: California requires training every 2 years for employers with 5+ staff, New York annual interactive training, and **Illinois restaurant-specific training in English and Spanish** ([ASE](https://www.aseonline.org/News-Events/Articles/what-states-and-jurisdictions-require-harassment-training-for-employees)). In the UK the Employment Rights Act 2025 has, since April 2026, required **6 years of holiday-pay records**. From **30 Oct 2026** it adds a duty to take "all reasonable steps" against sexual harassment, including by third parties. Shift-notice and cancellation-pay rules follow in 2027 ([VinciWorks](https://vinciworks.com/blog/employment-rights-act-timeline/)). The UK also requires a written fire risk assessment for every business since Oct 2023 ([Pilla](https://yourpilla.com/blog/fire-risk-assessment-hospitality)) and annual Gas Safe CP42 checks on commercial catering gas ([Hillingdon Council](https://www.hillingdon.gov.uk/gas-safety)).

## Recommended beachhead: US independent groups of 2–10 locations, with a free single-site tier

The bottom-up market is meaningful but not huge. About **749k US restaurants** exist, and **~70% are single-location operators** ([Orbital](https://withorbital.com/data/how-many-restaurants-in-the-us/)). Independents and small multi-unit operators hold about two-thirds of sales, roughly $740B ([NRN](https://nrn.com/top-500-restaurants/america-is-losing-independent-restaurants)). At $40–150 per location per month, US checklist and food-safety software is worth roughly **$360M–1.35B a year**. Great Britain's ~99k licensed venues ([NielsenIQ/CGA](https://nielseniq.com/global/en/news-center/2025/hospitality-numbers-rise-in-third-quarter-but-costs-crisis-and-budget-threaten-revival/)), plus cafés and takeaways, add an estimated £55–225M. These are inferences built from location counts and price points. Top-down analyst figures are not reliable here.

The recommended beachhead is **US independent restaurant groups with 2–10 locations**: full-service, fast-casual and café groups, ideally already on Toast or Square. Single-site operators come in through a free tier. The reasoning runs as follows. Multi-site owners feel the "I can't be everywhere" pain most acutely, so they value dashboards, corrective-action follow-through and handbook consistency, and they pay per location. Consolidation has pulled every dedicated vendor toward chains and franchisors, leaving this group underserved. US mid-market pricing is quote-based with implementation fees, so **transparent self-serve pricing is itself a differentiator in the US**. In the UK, Trail and Culinary Key already publish £33–£82.50 per-site prices. The UK is attractive second because SFBB explicitly accepts electronic diaries and the ERA 2025 creates fresh handbook and records demand. Entering it first would mean competing with Check65's consultancy content, Trail's Access distribution and Culinary Key's £33 floor at the same time.

Two segments are deliberately deprioritized. Ghost kitchens have high churn ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/chat/h/970edf22-e2e0-4aad-888b-a0e3452e9b0a/)), and food trucks bring tiny revenue per account. Franchisees with 5–50 units are a strong **secondary wedge** reachable through franchisee associations, though brand mandates can override their choice. Chains and franchisors are the right *later* market, because a franchisor mandate is the cheapest acquisition once won. The playbook is Jolt reaching >$20M ARR on ~$21.6M raised ([Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250818839282/en/Digi-Acquires-Jolt-to-Accelerate-ARR-Growth-and-Strengthen-SmartSense%E2%80%99s-Market-Leading-Offerings); [CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/company/jolt-2/financials)).

Manager dashboard & alerts** | Per-site completion, failures, open corrective actions and work orders; smart notifications (escalate only if not done) | Multi-site leaderboard, weekly owner email digest, anomaly flags (identical repeated readings, completed-in-seconds lists) | AI weekly ops summary, POS-linked context (sales versus labor versus failed checks) |

### Where it beats existing apps

The **first differentiator is the closed loop**. A fridge reads 46°F. The app requires a corrective action ("moved product, re-check in 30 min"). It auto-creates a work order on that specific asset from its QR record and texts the refrigeration vendor a link. It logs the visit cost against the unit's warranty, and it shows the whole chain in inspector mode. Xenia has work orders and corrective actions but no compliance depth or handbook. Trail has policies but no work orders. FoodDocs and Check65 have food-safety depth but no maintenance workflow. Linking these steps is where the $22,500 median repair spend and the $23,800 spoilage events become ROI the owner can see.

The **second is anti-pencil-whipping without mandatory hardware**. It uses live-capture-only photos with server timestamps and optional geofencing, flags for identical repeated readings and implausibly fast completion, auto-close at the deadline, and a "spot-check" prompt that asks a random item to be re-verified with a photo. Hardware becomes an upsell rather than a prerequisite. That directly answers the Bluetooth and printer complaints and the $2.5–4k sensor capex of ComplianceMate.

The **third is speed and resilience as product requirements**. Reviews show slow apps directly cause skipped checklists. Targets: under 2 seconds to open a checklist, under 1 second per item tap, full offline capture with background sync, and photo upload in the background. A shared kitchen tablet with a 4-digit PIN per employee fits real kitchens better than individual logins.

The **fourth is time-to-value under 15 minutes**. The owner picks a concept type (café, full-service, fast casual, bar), a location (which sets the rules profile), and their equipment. The app generates a starter set of checklists, logs, a maintenance register and a handbook draft. AI drafting helps here, as FoodDocs does for HACCP plans ([FoodDocs](https://www.fooddocs.com/post/fooddocs-raises-money)), but every AI output is a draft the operator approves.

The **fifth is commercial terms**. Unlimited users per location (SafetyCulture's per-user model punishes turnover), month-to-month with an annual discount, no implementation fee, and in-app chat support with a published response-time commitment. That counters the most emotional review complaints: lock-in and support that never fixes anything.

The **sixth is a bilingual frontline**. English and Spanish ship at launch, which Illinois already requires for restaurant harassment training. Per-user language settings come next, so the same checklist renders in each worker's language. The research flags multilingual demand as plausible but untested, so it is an open question as well as a feature.

AI belongs where POS platforms will not compete. Toast IQ already answers business questions and executes actions across ~148k locations ([StockTitan](https://www.stocktitan.net/news/TOST)), so a generic "AI assistant" is a losing position. The defensible AI features are compliance-specific: drafting HACCP plans, SOPs and handbooks from templates; answering staff questions grounded in the operator's own approved documents; grading photo evidence ("is the label present, is the line clean"); and summarizing the week's failures into an owner digest.

## Risks and open questions

The biggest structural risk is **customer fragility**. US independents fell 2.3% in 2025 to ~412.5k units ([NRN](https://nrn.com/top-500-restaurants/america-is-losing-independent-restaurants)), and closures alone will churn accounts. Generic B2B churn benchmarks of 5–10% a year are not restaurant-specific ([LiveX](https://www.livex.ai/benchmarks/churn-rate-saas)), so plan for worse. The mitigation is the accumulated audit record, which is the switching cost, plus integrations and multi-site accounts. The second risk is **platform encroachment**. Toast, Square or 7shifts could deepen their task modules, and Toast IQ already acts across locations. The mitigation is compliance depth and the maintenance loop, both of which sit outside POS data, and integrating with POS platforms rather than competing with them. The third risk is **exit economics**. Jolt sold at ~7x ARR to a strategic buyer, and Trail, Zenput and Squadle went to suites ([Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250818839282/en/Digi-Acquires-Jolt-to-Accelerate-ARR-Growth-and-Strengthen-SmartSense%E2%80%99s-Market-Leading-Offerings)). That argues for capital-efficient growth over venture-scale burn. The fourth is **regulatory liability**. If the app's thresholds are wrong for a jurisdiction, an operator could fail an inspection or cause harm. The mitigations are reviewed rules packs, visible rule sources, operator confirmation at setup, and clear terms that the operator remains responsible. The fifth is **scope creep**. Eight modules is a lot for a small team, which is why the MVP takes a thin slice of each pillar and makes it excellent rather than shipping every module broad.

## Conclusion

The research changes the framing of "build something like Check65." Check65 is a consultancy's distribution arm, and copying its feature list would put a startup into a contest with 30 years of UK regulatory content. The winnable product is something no incumbent has built for small operators: one fast, trustworthy record of what happened in the restaurant today, plus what broke, who fixed it, what it cost, and whether the staff knew the rules. Consolidation has made this gap wider over time. Every successful checklist vendor has been absorbed into a suite that sells to chains, which leaves the independent multi-site owner as the customer whose money is uncontested.

The strategic implication is to treat compliance as the wedge and maintenance as the moat. Food-safety logs get the app installed because inspectors and regulations demand them. The repairs and maintenance loop is the part the owner reads, because that is where the $22,500 a year and the $23,800 freezer failure live, and over time it builds an asset history that is painful to abandon. Build it capital-efficiently and integrate with POS platforms rather than fighting them, because the likeliest successful outcome is a strategic acquisition by one of those platforms.
