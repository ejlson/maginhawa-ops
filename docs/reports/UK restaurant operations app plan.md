# Win UK kitchens where Check65 can't follow

The UK opening is not another food-safety checklist app. **The self-serve price for digital SFBB and temperature logging has already fallen to about £19 per site per month on rolling terms** (Hubl, Lemon), with £4.99 diaries below that. The real opening is a single, fast, phone-first record of everything that decides an EHO's "confidence in management" score, an insurer's view of the premises, and an employment tribunal's view of the employer. That means food safety, opening and closing checks, repairs and maintenance tied to a statutory inspection calendar (gas, fire, TR19, EICR), and a handbook with employment records that keeps up with the Employment Rights Act 2025. Check65 is the field app of Food Alert's consultancy bundle. Food Alert is owned by Citation. The bundle is strong on regulatory content, advice lines and risk-assessment libraries. It is weak on price transparency, self-serve onboarding, and repairs and maintenance, and its public feature list shows no handbook. Trail, now owned by Access Group, is the visible mid-market incumbent at **£38–£82.50 per site per month on a 12-month minimum**. Hardware vendors such as Navitas lock buyers into 12–24 month contracts. The recommended beachhead is **independent and small-group (1–10 site) food-led pubs and restaurants in England and Wales**. Tenanted and leased pub estates (Star, Greene King, Admiral, Stonegate) and trade bodies (BII, UKHospitality) are the routes to scale, and the FSA's open FHRS data supports outbound targeting of 3- and 4-rated sites. Price should start at £19 to neutralise the anchor and earn its margin at about £39–£69 per site through the modules no £19 app has. Many statutory cadences and some legal thresholds below are flagged **[unverified]**. They must be checked against legislation.gov.uk, hse.gov.uk and FSA/FSS primary text before they become product rules or marketing claims.

## A crowded £19 floor, a locked-in middle and a consultancy top

The UK market splits into four tiers, and each one leaves a different gap. At the bottom, cheap self-serve SFBB and temperature apps cost **£5–£32 per site per month**: SFBB+, SFBB Pro, Hubl, Lemon, food-safety.app and Leafe. Most of these are 2024–26 startups adding AI features. In the middle sit checklist and ops SaaS products at **£32–£83 per site per month, usually with 12-month minimums**: Trail, Paddl and FoodDocs. Then come hardware-plus-software sensor vendors on 12–24 month contracts, Navitas and Checkit. At the top are consultancy-bundled platforms sold on quote only: Food Alert's Alert65 and Check65, and probably Shield Safety and NSF ([Hubl](https://hublapp.co.uk/best-food-safety-apps-uk/); [Lemon](https://www.getlemon.app/resources/blog/best-food-safety-apps-uk-kitchens-2026); [Trail pricing](https://trailapp.com/pricing)).

### Check65 is the mobile front door to a Citation retainer

Food Alert presents Check65 as the "due diligence" app. It turns paper checklists into tasks with real-time status and mirrors an organisation's regions, sites, departments and users. It records deliveries, hot and cold food checks and visitor logs, logs corrective actions, supports international time zones, and feeds dashboards that work with BI tools. **Bluetooth temperature checking is sold as a separate offer**, and no price appears on the page ([Food Alert](https://www.foodalert.com/due-diligence-in-the-palm-of-your-hand/)). The App Store listing adds daily, weekly and monthly scheduled lists (opening and closing, cleaning, fire safety), ad-hoc lists such as visitor logs, hot checks (cooking, reheating, hot holding) and cold checks (cooling, ambient, fridge, freezer) ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1611955849)). Behind it, Alert65 provides digital HACCP with **200+ templates**, **200+ consultant-written risk assessments** with version history, group policies that each site can customise, an allergen matrix and menu compliance, training tracking, certificate expiry reminders, RAG-scored accountability, complaints and incidents, and EHO visit preparation. It works offline and is ISO 27001 certified ([Food Alert Alert65](https://www.foodalert.com/alert65)).

The commercial model matters more than the feature list. Alert65 is sold in Independent, Multisite and Enterprise packages alongside compliance membership, audits, consultancy, a **24/7 advice line**, HACCP development, training and allergen analysis. Food Alert claims **3,000+ businesses**, including Fuller's and Wasabi ([Food Alert Alert65](https://www.foodalert.com/alert65)). The Citation Group bought Food Alert in 2019. Food Alert has since bought SureFoot, NT Assure, STS and Common Sense Compliance ([Food Alert](https://www.foodalert.com/food-alert-acquires-common-sense-compliance-ltd/); [Catering Today](https://www.cateringtoday.co.uk/news/regulations-policies/food-alert-acquires-surefoot-solutions-ltd)). Citation also sells HR, employment-law and health-and-safety retainers, so a combined "checklists + food safety + R&M + handbook" app competes with the whole Citation bundle, not just Check65. Food Alert runs PPC landing pages for the checklist app, which shows it wants standalone app buyers as well as consultancy clients ([Food Alert PPC](https://www.foodalert.com/checklists-app-ppc-v2/)). One more detail shows how the market has tangled: **Trail sells a "Trail + Surefoot" bundle at £105 per site per month**, and Surefoot is a Food Alert/Citation company ([Trail x Surefoot](https://trailapp.com/surefoot)). The inference that Check65 usually reaches customers inside multi-year retainers rests on Citation's model rather than on a published contract term, so treat it as **[unverified]**.

## UK operators are short-staffed, cost-squeezed and exposed on allergens

The pressures that shape what UK operators will buy all point the same way: fewer staff, higher costs, and more liability on each record. **Hospitality has the highest staff turnover of any UK sector at 38.7%, and 47% in bars and clubs** ([NatWest Mentor](https://www.natwestmentor.co.uk/news/staff-turnover-rates-by-industry-uk-2026-benchmarks)). Payrolled employees in accommodation and food services fell 4.1% to about 2.09 million in the year to August 2025 ([RSM UK](https://www.rsmuk.com/news/employment-in-hospitality-sector-continues-to-shrink)). Labour costs keep rising. The April 2025 employer NIC change pulled **774,000 hospitality workers into employer NICs, costing the sector about £1bn** ([Caterer Licensee](https://catererlicensee.com/774000-hospitality-workers-dragged-into-new-nics-threshold/)). The National Living Wage rose to **£12.71 from April 2026**, about £1.4bn of extra cost according to UKHospitality ([Restaurant Online](https://www.restaurantonline.co.uk/Article/2025/11/26/national-living-wage-to-increase-by-41-to-1271/)). Hospitality accounted for **53% of UK job losses** after the 2024 Budget ([UKHospitality](https://www.ukhospitality.org.uk/?p=8371)). The partial offset is the new permanently lower business-rates multiplier for retail, hospitality and leisure (RHL) premises in England from April 2026, which is 38.2p for rateable values under £51k ([Morning Advertiser](https://www.morningadvertiser.co.uk/Article/2025/10/17/government-confirms-new-lower-business-rates-multipliers-for-hospitality-from-april-2026)). It helps the small pubs and restaurants this plan targets most. Hotels face revaluation increases, which makes them a weaker first segment ([UKHospitality](https://www.ukhospitality.org.uk/?p=10308)). The product implication is that the same checks get done by fewer, newer people. Onboarding, task guidance that needs no training, and records that build themselves matter more in the UK than in low-churn sectors. Software sold as "nice to have" will lose to software that saves manager hours or protects the rating.

Allergens are where the stakes are sharpest. Councils prosecuted three allergen cases in 2025:

- **Javitri in Uxbridge paid £43,816** after a customer was hospitalised. Officers found nuts stored incorrectly, incomplete menu descriptions and "uncertainty regarding recipes", even though staff had completed FSA allergen training that month ([Hillingdon Council](https://www.hillingdon.gov.uk/article/14517/Uxbridge-restaurant-fined-more-than-40000-after-allergen-contamination-led-to-customer-being-hospitalised)).
- **Pop-Inn Café in Southwark was fined £18,000** after the death of 12-year-old Mia-Shay St Hilaire ([Southwark Council](https://www.southwark.gov.uk/news/2025/southwark-cafe-fined-food-safety-breaches-following-death-12-year-old-girl-allergic)).
- **A Hartlepool takeaway was fined £10,000** after a test purchase found peanut protein ([Hartlepool Council](https://www.hartlepool.gov.uk/news/article/107/takeaway-restaurant-fined-over-peanut-protein-in-meal)).

Javitri is the strongest product lesson in the research. Training alone fails without recipe-level allergen records tied to the live menu. The FSA's updated best-practice guidance for non-prepacked food in England, Wales and Northern Ireland now says written allergen information should always be available alongside a conversation with the customer. The law still permits verbal information ([FSA](https://www.food.gov.uk/news-alerts/news/updated-industry-guidance-issued-for-food-allergen-information-in-the-out-of-home-sector); [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/allergen-information-for-non-prepacked-foods-best-practice/allergen-information-for-non-prepacked-foods-best-practice)).

Complaints about the software itself carry over from the wider market. UK review data is thin: Trail has only seven Capterra UK reviews. They praise ease of use and support, and complain about a "little clunky" mobile app and reports that take up to 30 minutes ([Capterra UK](https://www.capterra.co.uk/software/176765/trail)). Larger cross-market review sets show the same pattern the UK reviews hint at. Jolt users call the mobile app "slow and sometimes freezes up" and warn others about contract terms "locking you in" ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/p/146384/Jolt/reviews/); [Software Advice](https://www.softwareadvice.com/logbook/jolt-profile/reviews/)). Crunchtime Ops reviewers cite slow image uploads, Bluetooth problems and "expensive hardware on top of monthly subscriptions" ([G2](https://g2.com/products/zenput/reviews?page=3)). SafetyCulture's value depends on staff actually completing photos and notes ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/p/141080/iAuditor/reviews/)). Underneath all of it is **pencil-whipping**, meaning logs completed without the work being done. Staff do it because they are short of time, poorly trained, and believe nobody reads the logs ([Crunchtime](https://www.crunchtime.com/blog/how-to-prevent-pencil-whipping); [Ladle](https://ladle.com/resources/blog/mitigate-checklist-falsification-at-your-restaurant-with-one-tool/)). Hardware is the usual fix and also the top complaint, because Bluetooth and Wi-Fi struggle through stainless steel and insulated cold units ([Refrigerated & Frozen Foods](https://www.refrigeratedfrozenfood.com/articles/94163-temperature-humidity-sensors-for-foodservice-kitchens?v=preview)).

It found no UK data on equipment breakdown frequency or contractor costs. It found no statistic on what share of operators still use paper SFBB diaries. The US analogue, a **median $22,500 a year on equipment repair and maintenance**, should not be quoted as a UK figure ([Bar & Restaurant](https://www.barandrestaurant.com/operations/new-report-empowers-operators-make-better-repair-maintenance-spending-decisions)).

## UK regulation rewards records, and the four nations differ in ways software must encode

### FHRS turns record-keeping into a public score

The Food Hygiene Rating Scheme in England, Wales and Northern Ireland rates businesses 0–5 on three elements: hygienic food handling, physical condition, and **confidence in management**, which covers food safety management systems, training and procedures ([GOV.UK/FSA](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/food-hygiene-rating-scheme/food-hygiene-rating-scheme)). Hygiene and structure are each scored 0/5/10/15/20/25. Confidence in management is scored **0/5/10/20/30**, and the worst band means no evidence of documented food safety management. A total of 0–15 earns a 5, and above 50 earns a 0 ([NCASS](https://www.ncass.org.uk/wp-content/Guides/Food%20Hygiene%20Rating%20Scheme%20Guide.pdf); [Reigate & Banstead](https://www.reigate-banstead.gov.uk/info/20376/food_hygiene_rating_scheme/199/food_hygiene_rating_scheme_fhrs_-_information_for_businesses)). An "additional scoring factor" lets a single bad element cap the overall rating ([High Speed Training](https://highspeedtraining.co.uk/hub/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/fhrs-scoring-table.pdf)). The exact per-element caps are **[unverified]**: the FSA Brand Standard PDF returned a 404, and the figures in the notes come from memory. Display is a **legal requirement in Wales and Northern Ireland** and voluntary in England ([GOV.UK/FSA](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/food-hygiene-rating-scheme/food-hygiene-rating-scheme)). Whether mandatory display has been legislated in England by October 2026 is **[unverified]**. A paid re-rating visit costs roughly **£175–£265**, depending on the council, and takes place within three months ([Erewash](https://www.erewash.gov.uk/sites/default/files/2025-03/FHRS%20Revisit%20Form.pdf); [Gloucester](https://www.gloucester.gov.uk/media/hpvjd2ny/fhrs-re-visit-with-fee.pdf)). The 21-day appeal window and right-to-reply safeguards are **[unverified]**. Scotland uses a different scheme, FHIS, with Pass or Improvement Required, and its details were not fetched **[unverified]**.

This scoring structure is the product's sharpest ROI argument. Confidence in management is driven almost entirely by documentation, and one weak element can cap the rating. A digital diary that is complete, reviewed and recognisable as SFBB maps directly onto a scoring component.

### SFBB explicitly accepts an electronic diary

The FSA's Safer Food Better Business packs come in versions for caterers, retailers, Indian cuisine, Chinese cuisine and childminders, plus a care-home supplement. Each covers cross-contamination, cleaning, chilling, cooking and management, with a diary for daily records and a **4-weekly review**. The FSA says the pack "must be completed for all your food operations" with daily records kept in the diary, and that businesses can keep an **electronic diary** ([GOV.UK/FSA](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/safer-food-better-business-sfbb/safer-food-better-business-sfbb)). The FSA has moved its business guidance from food.gov.uk to gov.uk, and old URLs now return 301 redirects, so any regulatory deep links in the app must point to gov.uk. The page-level content of each pack has not been checked: the opening and closing check lists, the safe-method names and the "prove it" checks. All of that, and CookSafe's contents for Scotland, is **[unverified]** and must be read from the PDFs before the templates are written.

The traditional minimum intervals are 6 months for A, 12 months for B, 18 for C, 24 for D, and an alternative strategy for E. The Food Standards Delivery Model has been statutory for English councils since 1 April 2025 ([FSA](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/fsa-250904-annual-local-authority-performance/fsa-250904-annual-local-authority-performance)). Enforcement is intensifying. In 2025/26, formal enforcement actions reached their highest yearly volume in more than ten years, complaints exceeded 64,000, and councils achieved 98.2% of due interventions for A- and B-rated premises ([FSA September 2026 Board](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/food-standards-agency-september-2026-board-meeting/local-authority-performance-update-food-standards-agency-september-2026-board-meeting)).

### Martyn's Law matters to large venues and to estates

The Terrorism (Protection of Premises) Act 2025 received Royal Assent on 3 April 2025 with an implementation period of at least 24 months, and the SIA is the regulator. Guidance published in 2026 covers statutory guidance, notification and "principal use" ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/terrorism-protection-of-premises-act-2025)). The **Standard tier (200–799 people, staff included)** requires public protection procedures for evacuation, invacuation, lockdown and communication, plus notification to the SIA. The **Enhanced tier (800+)** adds security measures, a documented plan and a designated senior individual ([Foot Anstey](https://www.footanstey.com/our-insights/articles-news/martyns-law-an-update-following-final-statutory-guidance/)). Standard-tier penalties reach £10,000 plus £500 a day ([Keoghs](https://keoghs.co.uk/keoghs-insight/martyns-law-government-guidance-published)). Enforcement is expected from spring 2027, but the exact commencement date is **[unverified]**. The sources also disagree on when the final statutory guidance was published: 15 April 2026 or 25 August 2026. Most restaurants hold fewer than 200 people. Large pubs, venues with function rooms and food halls do cross the threshold, which makes this a modest but real feature for pub estates. Store a capacity figure per site. At 200 or more, generate the notification task, the procedures template, staff briefing records and a review cycle.

## The opportunity: 98,600 licensed sites, 104,000 new food businesses a year, and an FHRS map to all of them

The widest universe is the **590,000 UK food establishments** the FSA regulates. Of those, **36,000 are unrated**, and **about 104,000 new food businesses registered in 2025/26, up 8.7%** ([FSA September 2026 Board](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/food-standards-agency-september-2026-board-meeting/local-authority-performance-update-food-standards-agency-september-2026-board-meeting)). That universe includes care homes, schools and retailers. The licensed on-trade core is far smaller: **98,564 GB licensed premises at the end of June 2026**, flat on the quarter. That figure hides heavy churn, with **1,839 closures and 1,794 openings in Q2 2026** alone, about 20 a day each way ([NIQ/CGA](https://nielseniq.com/global/en/news-center/2026/hospitality-outlets-flat-in-second-quarter-after-rapid-churn-of-closures-and-openings/)). Independents lost nearly a fifth of their sites in five years, but they grew 0.9% in Q3 2025 ([CGA](https://cgastrategy.com/?p=84949)). Bar numbers grew 1.4% year on year, and city centres outperformed the wider market ([NIQ](https://nielseniq.com/global/en/news-center/2026/hospitality-outlets-flat-in-second-quarter-after-rapid-churn-of-closures-and-openings/)).

A rough bottom-up estimate, which is an inference rather than a sourced figure, puts the addressable UK market at **about £34–£135M a year**. It takes roughly 150,000–250,000 food-service sites (licensed venues plus cafés, takeaways and contract catering) at £19–£45 per site per month. The site count is **[unverified]** because ONS SIC 56 local-unit counts were not extracted. That is a niche, not a venture-scale market on its own. It argues for capital efficiency and for the higher-value modules rather than pure food-safety logging.

FHRS data reshapes the sales pitch. **78% of UK businesses are rated 5 and 97% are rated 3 or above** ([FSA](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/food-standards-agency-september-2026-board-meeting/local-authority-performance-update-food-standards-agency-september-2026-board-meeting)). "Avoid a zero" therefore speaks to only about 3% of the market. High Speed Training's analysis of the FHRS API reaches a different picture. For England, Wales and NI combined it puts 72.33% at 5 and **11.69% at 3 or below**. It finds **19.5% of takeaways at 3 or below**, puts London **10th of 11 regions** with an average of 4.39, and counts 501 premises rated 0 ([High Speed Training](https://www.highspeedtraining.co.uk/hub/?p=79755)). The two sources measure different populations, so their figures differ. The commercial message that follows is "**get to 5, stay at 5, and prove it at the next visit**". It is aimed at the 3s and 4s, recently re-rated sites, new registrations and unrated businesses. Each rating is public and timestamped through the FSA's open API, so targeting is unusually precise ([FSA FHRS](https://www.food.gov.uk/about-us/food-hygiene-rating-scheme-fhrs)). Displayed stickers make the reputational stake visible. Only **5% of 0–2 rated English businesses display their sticker, against 81% of 5-rated ones** ([FSA Audit of Display 2025](https://science.food.gov.uk/article/162577-food-hygiene-rating-scheme-audit-of-display-and-business-survey-2025)).

### Recommended UK beachhead: food-led independents and small groups in England and Wales

The beachhead is **independent and small-group (1–10 site) food-led pubs and restaurants in England and Wales**. Takeaways come in as a self-serve secondary segment, and Scotland and NI follow once their rules packs are built. Five reasons support this choice. First, England and Wales share SFBB, the 8°C/63°C regime and FHRS, so one rules pack covers most of the market, while Scotland's CookSafe, 82°C reheat and FHIS need a separate pack. Second, food-led pubs and restaurants feel all four pillars at once: food safety, a building full of gas, extraction and electrics, staff turnover, and tipping. That is where a combined product justifies more than £19. Third, tenanted and leased pubs can be reached in bulk through pubcos, which have regulatory and brand reasons to monitor tenant compliance. Star Pubs & Bars already deployed Shield Safety's software with a traffic-light remote view ([Shield Safety](https://shieldsafety.co.uk/resources/case-studies/star-pubs)). Greene King Pub Partners spent **£200k on a licensee app** ([Caterer Licensee](https://catererlicensee.com/greene-king-pub-partners-invests-200000-in-app-to-strengthen-collaboration-with-licensees/)). Fourth, multi-site owners with 2–10 sites feel the "I can't be everywhere" pain and pay per site. Fifth, the Wales rule that ratings must be displayed sharpens the stakes there.

The main argument against this beachhead deserves weight. Takeaways have the worst ratings, and new registrations are the largest inflow, so a pure FHRS-improvement product might grow faster there. These segments, however, are exactly where £4.99–£19 apps already compete, and they have the lowest willingness to pay for maintenance or HR features. Serve them with an entry tier and outbound FHRS campaigns, but do not design the product around them. Hotels are deprioritised because of revaluation-driven cost spikes, and contract catering because it is dominated by enterprise buyers.

### Seven differentiators that beat Check65, Trail and the £19 apps

The **first differentiator is the closed loop from check to certificate**. A fridge reads 9°C at opening. In England that is above the 8°C legal limit **[unverified threshold]**. The app demands a corrective action, re-checks the unit in 30 minutes, and opens a work order on that specific fridge from its QR record. It texts the refrigeration contractor a link and logs the visit, cost and invoice against the asset. The whole chain then appears in the EHO pack under confidence in management. Check65 records corrective actions, Trail paywalls them at its £75 Standard tier ([Trail](https://trailapp.com/pricing)), and none of the £19 apps reaches a contractor.

The **second is the statutory calendar for the building**. No food-safety app in the UK research presents gas, TR19, EICR, fire and legionella obligations as one calendar with certificates and work orders. For a pub tenant, that is the difference between a food-safety app and the tool that runs compliance for the premises.

The **third is SFBB and CookSafe fidelity**. The diary, the 4-weekly review and the safe-method structure mirror the FSA pack, so an EHO recognises the system on sight. A Scotland profile encodes CookSafe, FHIS and the 82°C reheat rule **[unverified]**. Generic checklist tools and US entrants do not do this.

The **fourth is resistance to pencil-whipping without mandatory hardware**. The app accepts only live-capture photos, records server timestamps, flags identical repeated readings and implausibly fast completion, auto-closes lists at deadline, and prompts random spot checks. Probes and sensors become an upsell, not a 12–24 month hardware contract.

The **fifth is employment evidence for 2026–27**. Harassment prevention, third-party harassment, tipping consultation, holiday records and soon shift-notice records arrive on a fixed timeline. A handbook that updates, re-collects signatures and logs the evidence is a concrete reason to pay above £19. It also directly contests the part of the Citation bundle that Food Alert's app does not cover.

The **sixth is speed and fit for a real kitchen**. Targets are under 2 seconds to open a list, under 1 second per tap, full offline capture and background photo upload. A shared tablet with a 4-digit PIN per staff member suits a team that turns over 38–47% a year better than individual logins do.

The **seventh is commercial terms**. Prices are published in GBP, terms are rolling monthly, users are unlimited, there is no setup fee, and support runs in-app with a published response time. This answers the most emotional complaints in cross-market reviews, about lock-in and unresponsive support. It also contrasts directly with Trail's 12-month minimum and Navitas's 12–24 month terms.

AI belongs only where it is grounded and reviewable. That means drafting safe methods, HACCP and handbook sections from templates; answering staff questions from the operator's own approved documents; checking photo evidence; and summarising the week. Hubl's "Orbit" and Paddl's AI-drafted HACCP already make AI table stakes at the low end ([Hubl](https://hublapp.co.uk/); [Paddl](https://paddl-ai.co/best/best-food-safety-apps-uk)), so AI is not a differentiator on its own.

#### Ask your logs: the headline AI feature

Every check, temperature, corrective action, fault, work order, invoice, policy sign-off and certificate the app stores becomes **searchable and analysable in plain English**. Competitors give dashboards and exports. This gives every manager an analyst. It is also the AI feature that improves as the closed loop gathers data, so it is hard for a £19 checklist app to copy.

| Type | Example questions | What comes back |
|---|---|---|
| **Search** | "Show every time the walk-in was above 8°C in September." · "Who signed off the cooling log on 12 August?" · "Find the photo of the fryer fault last month." · "Any notes mentioning mice or droppings this year?" | The matching records with time, person, photo and corrective action, each linking to the original entry |
| **Analysis** | "Which site misses closing checks most, and on which days?" · "Is fridge 2 getting warmer week on week?" · "Which checklists are completed suspiciously fast?" · "How many corrective actions were still open at close this month?" | A short written answer plus a chart or table, with an "add to dashboard" option |
| **Money (Finance role)** | "How much have we spent on refrigeration repairs this year, by site?" · "Which contractor has the slowest response time?" · "Is it cheaper to replace the ice machine than keep repairing it?" | Cost totals, trends and cost per asset; exportable to Excel, Xero, QuickBooks or Sage |
| **Compliance** | "Are we ready for an EHO visit?" · "Which staff haven't signed the new harassment policy?" · "Which certificates expire in the next 60 days?" · "Build me the EHO pack for the last 3 months." | A gap list ranked by FHRS element; one-tap pack or reminder sends |
| **Explain** | "Why did yesterday's end-of-day report show 3 failures?" · "What changed at the Brixton site since its rating dropped?" | A plain-English explanation that cites the records it used |

**How it works, and how it stays safe:**

- **The AI calls the app's own query tools** (filter by site, date, check type, asset, person; aggregate; chart) rather than writing free-form database queries. Answers then always match the real data and respect what each role may see. Staff see only their own tasks; Finance sees costs but not, for example, staff health records.
- **Every answer cites its records.** If the data does not answer the question, it says so rather than guessing.
- **Free-text notes, photos and voice notes are indexed for meaning** (semantic search), so "mice" also finds "droppings behind the dry store".
- **Saved questions become scheduled reports.** For example, "every Monday, email me last week's temperature failures by site". This replaces most hand-built reports, and feeds the end-of-day report and the spreadsheet summary from the founding team's notes.
- **Answers can be exported or pinned.** Any answer can go to Excel/CSV, into the EHO pack, or onto the dashboard.

**How to build it.** Use a hosted LLM API with vision, such as Anthropic's Claude: a larger model for document and photo understanding and drafting, and a small fast model for classification and translation. Keep retrieval scoped to each operator's own documents. Store prompts, sources and outputs alongside the audit trail, so every AI suggestion can be traced. Process data under UK GDPR with a data processing agreement and no training on customer data. Cap AI usage per plan so the £19 tier stays profitable. A sensible split is snap-to-setup and ask-the-handbook in every tier, with the mock inspection, early warnings and allergen assistant in the £39+ tiers.

## Price at the £19 anchor, earn margin on the building and the people

Clear published prices make the UK pricing landscape easy to read. The **self-serve food-safety anchor is about £19 per site per month on rolling terms** (Hubl, Lemon). Trail's entry is £38 (£32 billed annually) with a 12-month minimum, and the BII member rate is £12 after three free months ([Trail BII](https://trailapp.com/group/bii)). Navitas reportedly charges £129–£159 on 12–24 month contracts (3P). Rota tools charge about £1 per employee per month ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.co.uk/software/137099/rotacloud)). A combined app replacing two or three tools can plausibly charge £35–£60, but buyers will compare it line by line with £19 food-safety apps. Two levers make the maths easier. An insurer discount like the earlier **Towergate–Checkit deal of £60–£100 per site per year** offsets £5–£8 a month, which is a third to almost half of Essentials. A **£175–£265 FHRS re-visit fee** avoided, or a single allergen prosecution like Javitri's £43,816, dwarfs a year of subscription. The churn data also argues for rolling terms. About 1 in 14 licensed sites closes each year, so lock-in contracts mostly create bad debt and resentment, not retention.

### UK go-to-market: five channels in priority order

**Pub company estate deals** give the most leverage, because one contract brings many sites and the pubco has a reason to pay. Shield Safety's Star Pubs deal gives licensees audits, an EHO helpline and mobile software, and lets Star monitor compliance remotely. Shield says checks take 30–60 seconds ([Shield Safety](https://shieldsafety.co.uk/resources/case-studies/star-pubs)). Greene King's £200k licensee app shows pubcos will fund tenant-facing tools ([Caterer Licensee](https://catererlicensee.com/greene-king-pub-partners-invests-200000-in-app-to-strengthen-collaboration-with-licensees/)). The pitch to a pubco is building compliance across the estate (gas, TR19, fire, EICR) plus FHRS protection, and that pitch is stronger than food safety alone. No evidence was found that Admiral or Stonegate currently mandate an app, so that is open ground **[unverified]**.

**Trade-body member pricing** comes second: the BII, UKHospitality and NCASS for street food. Trail's BII offer shows the format (3 months free, then a discounted rate) ([Trail BII](https://trailapp.com/group/bii)). **EPOS and rota marketplaces** come third. Trail integrates with Epos Now and Deputy, Epos Now reportedly lists 150+ apps **[unverified]**, and Access claims "1,500+ apps" across its suite ([Trail BII](https://trailapp.com/group/bii); [Access Group](https://www.theaccessgroup.com/en-gb/about/news/the-access-group-acquires-trail/)). Marketplace terms for Lightspeed, Square UK, Zonal and SumUp were not researched. **Insurers** come fourth: a premium discount for documented compliance, with TR19 and gas certificates as the evidence insurers already ask for. Only the historic Towergate–Checkit deal is evidenced. Hiscox, Simply Business, Markel and NFU Mutual were not checked. **FHRS open-data outbound** comes fifth and runs continuously. Pull 3- and 4-rated sites, newly rated sites and unrated new registrations from the FSA API, then send "get to 5 and prove it" campaigns by postcode, with London and takeaways as high-yield pockets ([High Speed Training](https://www.highspeedtraining.co.uk/hub/?p=79755)). Food hygiene trainers such as High Speed Training, and independent EHP consultants, double as content partners and as the advice layer this plan deliberately does not build.

## Risks and the questions operators must answer

The biggest risk is **bundle competition from above**. Citation can sell food safety, HR, employment-law advice and H&S as one retainer. Access can bundle Trail with EPOS, rota and property maintenance. Either could add a work-order module. The mitigation is to win on what bundles do badly: speed, self-serve onboarding, rolling terms and a single building-and-people record that a small operator can set up in 15 minutes. The second risk is **price compression from below**. £19 AI-equipped startups appear every year, so Essentials must stay competitive and the margin must come from the R&M, calendar and handbook tiers. If operators will not pay £39 for that bundle, the thesis fails, and this is the first thing to test. The third is **customer fragility**. About 1,800 licensed sites close every quarter, and independents remain the most volatile segment. Pubco estate contracts and multi-site groups dampen this. The fourth is **regulatory liability**. A wrong threshold or cadence could mislead an operator, and many of the rules here are **[unverified]**. The mitigation is versioned rules packs signed off by an EHP, visible sources inside the app, editable defaults, and terms that keep the operator responsible. The fifth is **scope creep into HR**. Payroll, rota and full HR belong to RotaCloud, Deputy, BrightHR and Citation. Integrate with them rather than rebuilding them. Further risks follow. Category exits have priced at mid-single-digit ARR multiples: Digi bought Jolt for about $145.5M on more than $20M ARR ([Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250818839282/en/Digi-Acquires-Jolt-to-Accelerate-ARR-Growth-and-Strengthen-SmartSense%E2%80%99s-Market-Leading-Offerings)). With a UK market of perhaps £34–£135M a year, that argues for capital-efficient growth.

## Conclusion

The UK research changes the plan in one important way. The £19 rolling-term apps have commoditised food-safety logging, so a UK entrant that leads with "digital SFBB" is entering a price war it cannot win. The defensible product treats the FHRS confidence-in-management score as the hook, then becomes the compliance record for the whole premises and workforce: the gas certificate, the TR19 clean, the fridge that failed and who fixed it, the harassment policy every new starter signed, and the tipping consultation. Those are the records no £19 app holds. Check65 holds them only inside a consultancy retainer. Trail holds them only inside a 12-month contract and the Access suite.

The second insight concerns channels. In the UK the buyer and the payer are often different organisations. A pubco, an insurer or a landlord has more reason to pay for building-level compliance evidence than a tenant does to pay for temperature logs. Pricing that starts at the market's £19 anchor, combined with estate and insurer deals that move the cost off the operator, is the most credible route past both Citation's bundle and the low-cost crowd. That route depends on getting the four nations' rules right, which is why verifying the **[unverified]** items is the first engineering task, not the last.
